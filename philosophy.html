Teaching

Philosophy

Evidence-based reasoning, real-world problem solving, and learning through peer feedback.

Approach

Three principles

My teaching is grounded in three connected ideas: making evidence-based reasoning visible, connecting theory to real-world problems, and helping students learn from one another through iterative feedback. An interdisciplinary path — from design practice to environmental psychology, organizational behavior, and mixed-method research — has shown me that students often enter the same room with different ways of seeing a problem. I treat teaching as guided exploration: scaffolding complex work into understandable steps, demonstrating multiple ways to engage with evidence, and asking students to explain not only what they find, but why the evidence supports their conclusions.

In practice, students learn by doing and by teaching one another — framing a problem, reviewing prior research, collecting or interpreting evidence, testing explanations, exchanging peer comments, and revising their arguments. Clear expectations and concrete examples make methods accessible; real-world problem solving and repeated critique ask students to take responsibility for their reasoning and learn from classmates with different expertise. These habits hold across research seminars and design studios alike.

Experience

Instructor and studio

From 2023 to 2026, teaching became central to my graduate work. Across more than seven courses — research seminars, project-based studios, and technical classes — I designed and taught an undergraduate behavioral research course and supported studios in workplace strategy, design thinking, user experience, and design computing. Student projects and course feedback continue to shape how I refine the approach.

As graduate instructor of record for Introduction to Environmental Psychology — Writing in the Major (Fall 2025), with mentorship from Professor Gary Evans, I guided eight teams through a fourteen-week arc from observation to empirical papers and presentations: research questions, literature, construct definition, methods, field data, analysis, visualization, and evidence-based recommendations. Staged submissions, practice presentations, written peer comments, and consulting-style client presentations made empirical reasoning visible and gave students room to revise. Campus settings such as Cornell’s eHub and the Botanic Gardens connected theory to stakeholders who could act on the findings. Feedback from that course — including strong ratings for responsiveness and examples — led me to add clearer rubrics, annotated models, and workshops before major deadlines: the same principle I ask of students, that feedback should inform the next iteration rather than only judge finished work.

In project-based teaching assistantships — including Design UX with Technology Studio, Workplace Strategy Studio, and Studies in Design Thinking — I help teams translate ambiguous client and user needs into questions that can be examined through research, critique, and evidence-based design or managerial recommendations. Heuristic questions are a core tool: What assumption is this proposal making? What evidence supports it? What alternative explanation or user perspective is missing? How could someone act on this recommendation? Critiques make disciplinary expertise visible and useful, so students leave able to identify a problem, distinguish evidence from assumption, work with people who think differently, and communicate recommendations others can use.

Looking ahead

Curriculum and mentorship

I am prepared to teach human–computer interaction and computer-supported cooperative work, tying sociotechnical theory and research on environmental psychology and team dynamics to short cases and accessible datasets, and asking students to show what evidence supports an interpretation. In project courses I bring interviews, field observation, usability evaluation, and peer critique into real-world design work — so students practice evidence-based reasoning by analyzing collaborative work, then applying that evidence to design decisions. I am also ready to contribute to research methods teaching, drawing on mixed-methods field research.

An advanced elective on hybrid teamwork and emerging technologies would extend my dissertation research on how physical workplaces, digital collaboration tools, and generative AI relate to team experience, coordination, and performance. Rather than a conventional final alone, I favor interdisciplinary sprints in which students collaborate across fields, use AI-enabled tools, learn from industry mentors, exchange critique, and develop tangible prototypes from observed design challenges — bringing investigation, peer learning, and revision into one arc.

In mentoring undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral researchers around hybrid teamwork, technology-supported coordination, and the future of work, I match responsibilities to training: beginning researchers assist with reviews, observations, interviews, and coding; more experienced students develop questions, lead analyses, and evaluate prototypes. Clear expectations, regular feedback, peer exchange, and shared standards for ethics, documentation, reproducibility, and authorship make interdisciplinary research accessible and help students build independent judgment. Where possible, partnerships with organizations and communities keep field research connected to practical design recommendations.
